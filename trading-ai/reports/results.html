Can a trading model make 33% a month?

No — and the work says so three different ways. The arithmetic puts a floor of Sharpe 2.62 on the target before leverage is even a question. The simulation says the leverage the arithmetic calls for liquidates the account. And the sealed holdout says the edge, while still measurable, has shrunk to about the size of the trading fees.

The two Sharpe figures come from the same model, the same frozen configuration and the same cost assumptions. The only difference is which months they cover. Everything that follows is an attempt to explain that gap, because it is the whole answer.

Why the answer is arithmetic before it is empirical

Leverage rescales an edge; it does not create one. A book run at annualised volatility s with Sharpe S compounds at S·s − s²/2 — the return grows linearly in leverage but the volatility drag grows with its square. That expression peaks at s = S, so the best compound growth any book can reach, at any leverage, is S²/2 per year.

Turning that around: 33% a month requires Sharpe ≥ 2.62 (√(24 · ln 1.33)). Below that the target is not a question of how much leverage the exchange allows — it is unreachable at every leverage. Above it, the target fixes the volatility, and the volatility fixes the gross notional and the drawdowns.

What the model is

A gradient-boosted ensemble ranks roughly — liquid USDT perpetual contracts every four hours and holds a dollar-neutral, factor-neutral long/short book. It does not forecast the direction of crypto; it forecasts which contracts will out- or under-perform their peers over the next few hours, from ~110 features spanning vol-normalised and residual momentum, taker order-flow imbalance, trade-intensity microstructure, funding and open-interest positioning, and market-state conditioning.

Training is expanding-window walk-forward, retrained quarterly, with a purge and a 48-bar embargo before every test window. Fills are at the next bar's open and pay exchange fees, half the spread, and square-root impact in the participation rate against that bar's actual dollar volume; funding is settled every eight hours on realised rates; delisted contracts are force-liquidated with penalty costs.

Why leverage, not a bigger volatility target, is the binding constraint

A dollar-neutral book of ~100 perpetuals has only single-digit annualised volatility per unit of gross notional: the names diversify each other, which is the whole point of the construction. So the growth rate is set by how much gross notional the venue will carry, not by the volatility target in the config.

Each row raises the gross-notional cap and lets volatility targeting run to it. Maintenance margin and bankruptcy are checked every bar, so a row marked liquidated is a path that would have been closed out, not a deep drawdown.

The ceiling the simulation actually reaches

The closed form is an upper bound that assumes independent lognormal increments. Run on the real return path, with maintenance margin and bankruptcy checked every bar, leverage stops paying well before the arithmetic says it should — and then it stops abruptly.

33%/monthnot reachedthe leverage the arithmetic calls for liquidates the book

The gap is the price of fat tails. The growth formula treats each hour as an independent draw; a real crypto return stream clusters its worst hours together, so the drawdown that arrives at high leverage is deeper than lognormal maths predicts — deep enough to cross the maintenance-margin line, at which point the position is closed for you and the compounding argument ends.

What would break this

  • Fill assumptions. The cost model charges a quarter of the flow at maker fees. A book that actually crosses the spread on every order pays the conservative column, not the base one.
  • Capacity. The edge lives in the mid-cap half of the universe. The capacity table shows where the participation cap starts eating the return; past that point the strategy is a different, worse strategy.
  • Leverage availability. The operating point assumes the venue carries the stated gross notional across ~100 contracts at once, through a liquidation cascade, without raising margin requirements. Exchanges raise them exactly when you least want it.
  • Crowding. Short-horizon cross-sectional reversal in crypto is not a secret. The IC-by-year table is the thing to watch: if the edge is being competed away, it shows up there first.
  • Regime dependence. The sample contains one full bull/bear cycle. It does not contain a sustained low-volatility, low-funding crypto market, because one has not happened yet on perpetuals.
